\section{Introduction}
Identical clinical notes can carry different specialty labels. We ask:
\emph{How does cross-label duplication affect the evaluation and prediction of
medical specialties from clinical text?} We distinguish new records of known
notes from unseen notes, compare three model families, and test a training-only
label-exclusion decoder. Collection-specific decision rules can limit
transfer~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}; weighting and augmentation provide
additional comparisons.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Data Analysis}
Structural CSV repairs retain 2,669 cases and supplied labels across 12
specialties. Surgery contributes 893 cases (33.5\%), versus 25 for Dermatology
(0.9\%). Cross-label transcription duplicates affect 1,528 cases (57.2\%);
their descriptions and titles are also identical, including 72/79 Neurosurgery
cases. Thus, imbalance and label ambiguity are distinct limitations. We retain
incomplete cases and exclude \texttt{keywords}: all 2,219 nonempty values start
with the target specialty.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}
For four predefined minority classes ($n<100$: Dermatology, Neurosurgery,
Ophthalmology, Psychiatry-Psychology), one variant deletes
$\max(1,\lfloor0.2m\rfloor)$ of $m$ isolated, case-insensitive \emph{the}
tokens from each eligible training transcription, adapting random
deletion~\citep{wei2019eda}. Titles
and other words remain intact; a SHA-256 seed uses seed~0, fold and record ID.
Invalid/empty variants are skipped. Conditions are uniform originals (A),
balanced originals (B), uniform deletion (C), balanced deletion (D), and balanced
matched copies (E). D/E share sources and addition counts. Per protocol,
864/872 source visits yield variants, deleting 1.24\% of their word tokens;
clinical meaning preservation is unvalidated.

\section{Models}
Three scikit-learn families are compared~\citep{pedregosa2011scikit}.
Multinomial Naive Bayes (NB) implements the provided baseline: lowercased,
Porter-stemmed \texttt{description}, TF-IDF, $\alpha=1$ and empirical priors.
Logistic regression (LR) and linear support vector classification (SVC) provide
n-gram baselines~\citep{wang2012baselines}. They use separately L2-normalised
\texttt{sample\_name}/\texttt{transcription} TF-IDF blocks, giving nonzero field
blocks equal norm. Exploratory LR ablation favoured this pair; NB differs in
representation and classifier.

For each query, exclusion (EX) masks classes attached to exact training matches
and selects the highest-scoring remaining class. Matching always uses
\texttt{sample\_name}/\texttt{transcription}, including for NB, lowercased with
normalised whitespace; entirely empty inputs are
ignored. The index uses original training cases only. No match or all
classes blocked falls back to raw prediction. A corpus hypothesis motivates EX:
additional records use labels not previously assigned to that input. This is
not a clinical constraint.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We compare five-fold \texttt{StratifiedKFold} over rows (R) with
\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} over notes (G), both shuffled with seed~0.
G groups normalised nonempty transcriptions, using titles when missing and
unique IDs for entirely empty inputs. Each protocol shares folds across
conditions, evaluating seven configurations with/without EX. Features, weights,
augmentation and exclusion indices use training
folds only; unchanged validation originals yield pooled out-of-fold accuracy,
macro F1 and per-class metrics.
R admits known notes; G tests unseen notes. EX must therefore be inactive in G.
Field, parameter and decoder choices are exploratory~\citep{cawley2010overfitting}.
Selection prioritises R accuracy subject to the project's minimum F1 $\geq25\%$,
then macro/minimum F1; otherwise macro F1 leads. The official accuracy target
is $>49\%$. G remains a separate transfer
check. The official 398-case test only generates predictions; neither protocol
measures its performance.

\subsection{Parameters/Hyperparameters}
LR/SVC use lowercased word unigram/bigram TF-IDF, sublinear term frequency,
\texttt{min\_df=2} (minimum document frequency), no stemming/stopword removal,
L2, $C=0.25$ (inverse
regularisation) and at most 2,000 iterations. LR uses multinomial log loss;
SVC uses one-versus-rest squared hinge. Augmentation is tested only with LR.
Each arm refits TF-IDF including its added texts. Balanced weights $N/(12n_c)$
use original training size $N$ and class counts $n_c$. Every original/child receives its class
weight, then weights sum to $N$ in every arm. Added rows still increase their
sources' relative influence. D/E match counts and weights, while lexical
changes can alter TF-IDF.

\section{Discussion}
Surgery to Orthopedic is the most frequent remaining confusion (67 cases).
\ErrorDiscussion{}
EX predicts an unobserved annotation conditioned on already observed labels,
not a medically determined distinction between identical notes. Its gains
depend on collection structure; future repetitions of an existing label could
violate the assumption. Grouped and row-wise estimates answer different
questions, so their difference is not an algorithmic improvement. Exact grouping
does not eliminate approximate duplicates, patient dependence or selection bias.
The deletion result concerns one conservative transformation, not augmentation
in general.

\section{Future Work}
We would test exclusion on independent collections and adjudicated multi-label
notes, separating annotation completion from specialty recognition. Nested
selection and medically grounded lexical augmentation would address selection
uncertainty and limited textual diversity.

\paragraph{LLM use.}
Codex assisted implementation, literature search, analysis and writing;
an external Claude review prompted the protocol/decoder audit. Augmentation
uses deterministic local rules, without LLM-generated clinical cases.
